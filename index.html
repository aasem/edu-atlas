The Governing Question

What kind of being does education presume the learner to be, and given that being, is education the formation of a person or the transmission of knowledge, toward what end, and on whose authority?

Every philosophy of education carries a picture of the human being, and that picture comes first. It decides what education is for, and the purpose then decides what is taught and who may teach it. Mencius and Xunzi disagree about the curriculum because they first disagree about human nature. So do Augustine and Pelagius, and Rousseau and the Church. The question is long because each clause depends on the one before it.

The picture of the person has two sides, and they vary independently. One is moral: is the learner good, bad, mixed, neither, or unfinished? That answer decides whether education cultivates what is there or corrects it. The other is the learner's drive: longing, love, curiosity, the wish for honour, the need for discipline, the will to make oneself, or the hope of reward. That answer decides what education can rely on to move the learner. Augustine's learner is restless and fallen; Rousseau's child is good and, for a long time, quiet. Each entry records both sides.

The picture is often unstated. Xunzi says outright that human nature is bad. A madrasa curriculum built on memorization, commentary and the teacher's licence to transmit (ijāza) never states its view of memory, intellect and the heart, but it implies one. Each entry therefore asks what the text says about the person and also what its practice assumes.

The end is the third thing, and here the order of the question matters. For most traditions the end is part of the nature. Aristotle's ergon, the characteristic work of a human being, belongs to what a human being is; fiṭra is already turned toward God; Mencius's sprouts are already sprouts of humaneness. For these thinkers nature and purpose are one thing seen from two sides. Modernity took the end out of the picture of nature, beginning when the new science set aside final causes. After that the purpose had to come from outside: from the state, from the market, or from each person's own choice. Only then did it become possible to fix the purpose first and assume whatever person fits it, as Macaulay's Minute of 1835 did for an empire and the economists' ‘human capital’, made central to economics in the early 1960s, does for the market. Each entry therefore also asks where the end comes from.

Tradition held nature and purpose together. Modernity separated them and left two options: to impose a purpose on a nature that has none, or to follow a nature that points nowhere. The bureaucratic school takes the first course and progressive teaching the second. Both are modern, and the atlas asks whether either can succeed.

The atlas cannot settle what human nature is, and does not try. What it can show is checkable: that each tradition answered the question, and that its answer shaped what it taught and how. The same test can be put to any curriculum or national education policy. Does it say what kind of being the learner is, or only what role the learner is to fill, such as citizen or manpower? A role is not a nature.

On the tradition

India: The Self Already There

The Upanishads begin from a learner who is already, in principle, what he is to become. The self is identical with the ground of all things, and what stands in the way is ignorance, not incapacity. This explains the most striking move in the Indian material: the Mundaka classes the Vedas themselves as lower knowledge. Yet the institution built around those texts was one of the most transmission-heavy the world has known. Pupils lived in the teacher's household and memorized the Veda syllable by syllable, preserving it orally with extraordinary accuracy for three thousand years. The two fit together: memorization keeps the vessel intact, and a teacher in whom the knowledge is realized makes it live.

Buddhism changes the person. There is no hidden self to recognize, only craving and ignorance to be dissolved, and the teaching is a raft to be left behind. The Nyāya logicians give India a theory of honest and dishonest debate. The Gita places teaching inside a crisis of action. Running through all of it is a tension that is the Indian form of the question of authority: a metaphysics of universal identity coexisted with institutions that restricted who could learn, by caste and by sex.

On the tradition

China: Cultivating the Person

Learning (xue) is the first word of the Analects, and no tradition makes it more central to being human. The Chinese debate about the person is also the clearest anywhere. Mencius says human nature is good, Xunzi that it is bad, Gaozi that it is neither, and each view brings its own curriculum: protect the sprouts, steam and bend the wood, or carve as one pleases. The Daoists dissent from learning as such and treat it as a loss of original simplicity.

Two thousand years later, Neo-Confucianism relived the quarrel in other terms. Zhu Xi taught that one investigates principle in things and texts, and Lu Xiangshan and Wang Yangming that it is found in the heart. The institutions were academies (shuyuan) and, above all, the civil service examinations, which from the Sui dynasty to 1905 tied the cultivation of the self to selection by the state. For thirteen centuries China ran the largest experiment ever made in joining formation to an examination, and the results bear on every modern system of credentials.

On the tradition

Islam: Knowledge, Adab and the Heart

The Islamic tradition has the richest vocabulary for the governing question: ʿilm (knowledge), taʿlīm (instruction), tarbiya (rearing), taʾdīb (formation in adab), and for the person fiṭra (the original disposition), ʿaql (intellect), qalb (heart) and nafs (the lower self). It also has two inheritances. One is prophetic and legal, carried by chains of transmission (isnād) and by the personal licence (ijāza) a teacher gives a student to teach a text. The other is Greek, received through Fārābī and Miskawayh, in which the person is a rational soul whose intellect must be actualized and whose natures differ, so that teaching must be graded. Ibn Rushd's three kinds of assent and Ibn Ṭufayl's solitary philosopher belong to that line.

Al-Ghazālī joined the legal, theological and Sufi strands into a single account in which knowledge is for action and the heart is a mirror to be polished under a shaykh. The madrasa, spread by the Nizāmiyya colleges from 1067, gave the tradition its institution, and Zarnūjī's manual its student's handbook. Ibn Khaldūn then described the whole system as a craft that forms habits in a civilization, with a naturalism no other pre-modern tradition matched. The question of authority runs through the tradition in three forms: the chain of transmission, the dispute with the Taʿlīmiyya over an infallible teacher, and the philosophers' practice of writing in concealment.

On the tradition

Judaism and Christianity: Study and the Inner Teacher

These are two models, and neither simply follows the Islamic one. In the medieval period Maimonides, Ibn Rushd and Aquinas share Aristotle and read one another, and the questions of graded teaching and concealment are common to all three. But rabbinic Judaism made study itself a form of worship and argument a form of study. It handed down the Torah through a chain of teachers and paired every student with a companion. It preserved the losing opinion beside the winning one, as Islamic law preserved the disagreements of its schools.

Christianity added a different problem. For Augustine the learner is fallen and the only true teacher is inward, so the question of the teacher becomes a question about grace. Monastic reading, Hugh of St Victor's restoration of the divine image, and Aquinas's teacher who assists nature as a doctor does are three answers to it. The universities of Paris and Bologna, born around 1200, are the institution that the modern section inherits.

On the tradition

Greece, Rome and the Renaissance: Habit, Speech and the Unfixed Self

The Greek word for this whole subject, paideia, meant upbringing, culture and education at once. Two lines came out of Athens and still shape Western schooling. The philosophical line ran through Aristotle, for whom virtue is a habit completed by practical wisdom and education the business of the lawgiver. The rhetorical line ran from Isocrates to Quintilian, for whom the educated person is a good man skilled in speaking. The medieval liberal arts combined them.

The Renaissance added a third line. Pico della Mirandola's Oration (1486) gave the West its first full statement of a learner with no fixed nature: God tells Adam that he has no settled place or form and will shape himself, sinking to the brutes or rising to the divine. Pico's freedom is still a ladder toward God, so his unfinished self is not yet Sartre's. A century later Montaigne turned the humanist inheritance toward the forming of judgment.

On the tradition

Modernity: The Order Reversed

Modern education begins with a change in the picture of nature. Bacon's Advancement of Learning (1605) set the end of learning as the relief of man's estate and set aside final causes as barren. Nature, human nature included, became a set of capacities with no end built in, and Locke's white paper applies that picture to the child. Once the end was no longer in the nature, it had to come from outside. The state took formation over from churches and households: Prussia's general school regulations of 1763 are an early landmark, and Fichte's Addresses to the German Nation (1808) made national education a programme for fashioning the will. The university justified itself first by Humboldt's story of self-formation, which still placed the end inside the person, and then by the story of a people's emancipation. Weber expelled formation from the lecture hall, and Lyotard reported that both stories had lost their hold.

For the colonized world modernity arrived as Macaulay's Minute: an education designed for the needs of an empire, with a person assumed to fit them. In South Asia it produced the lasting split between madrasa and school that Sir Syed, Iqbal and Fazlur Rahman tried in different ways to heal. In its sharpest form the reversal is the doctrine of human capital, set out by Theodore Schultz and Gary Becker in the early 1960s, in which the market sets the purpose of education and the learner is valued by his expected return.

Modernity also holds the opposite order. Rousseau, Pestalozzi, Froebel and Dewey claimed to fit the school to the child's nature rather than the child to the school. But the nature they followed had also lost its end, so following nature became following interest, and Dewey's growth has no end beyond further growth. The third modern source of purpose is the individual: Nietzsche's self that is a task, and Sartre's claim that there is no human nature, only what each person makes of himself. The responses fall into three groups. Newman, al-Attas and MacIntyre return to formation within a tradition. Freire, Illich and Rancière seek emancipation from the teacher and the school. Arendt defends the teacher's authority as responsibility for an old world handed to newcomers. Ngũgĩ adds that all of this happens in a language, and asks whose.

Question 1 of 7

Anthropology

What is a human being?

Is the learner good, bad, mixed, neither, or unfinished? What does the text state, and what does its practice assume?

The person has two sides, and the traditions answer them separately. The first is moral, and it decides whether education cultivates or corrects. Mencius, the Upanishads, Wang Yangming, Rousseau and Tagore find a good nature and ask the teacher to tend it and not to force it. Xunzi and Augustine find a nature that must be straightened or healed. Most traditions hold some version of a mixed view: the Gita's imperishable self tangled in the strands of nature, the rabbis' two inclinations, al-Ghazālī's heart and lower self, Kant's predisposition to good beside a propensity to evil. Aristotle, Locke and the Analects hold that the learner is neither good nor bad by nature and is made by habit. A fifth answer says the learner is unfinished: Pico's Adam has no fixed place and shapes himself, Iqbal's khudi is achieved through striving and can be lost, Freire calls human beings unfinished, and Sartre denies that there is a human nature at all. Iqbal makes this answer native to the Islamic tradition rather than imported into it.

The two sides vary independently. Mencius and Xunzi were both Confucians who prized learning; they disagreed on the moral side and arrived at opposite pedagogies, tending sprouts against steaming wood straight. Their disagreement is the governing question in miniature.

The most surprising agreements cross traditions. The Upanishadic self beneath names and forms, Augustine's inner teacher, al-Ghazālī's rusted mirror and Wang Yangming's innate knowing are versions of one view, in which the truth is within and veiled. Aristotle's hexis, Ibn Khaldūn's malaka, the Xueji's uncarved jade and Dewey's plasticity are versions of another. The deepest disagreement is about equality. Medieval philosophers in all three Abrahamic traditions, and Śaṅkara with his qualified pupil, assume that natures differ and teaching must be graded. Confucius, who said that in teaching there are no categories of person, Mencius, for whom anyone can become a sage, and Rancière, for whom intelligence is equal, assume the opposite. Neither side proves its assumption.

Question 2 of 7

Purpose

What is knowledge for?

Does that end belong to the learner's nature, or come from the state, the market or the learner's own choice? And is education the formation of a person or the transmission of knowledge?

Pre-modern traditions place the end of education beyond the learner's present self: liberation in India, sagehood in China, nearness to God in Islam, Judaism and Christianity, and in Greece happiness as the excellent activity of the soul. None of them adds this end to the nature from outside. Aristotle's ergon, the work of a human being, belongs to what a human being is; fiṭra is already turned toward God; the Upanishadic self already is what liberation reveals. All but one of the pre-modern texts in the atlas place the end within the nature. The exception, Pico, gives the choice to the learner while keeping the direction given.

Modernity separated nature and purpose. Bacon set final causes aside as barren, and by Weber's lecture of 1917 science could say how to master life but not how to live it. A nature with no end built into it is a bundle of capacities, and its purpose must come from outside: from the state (Locke's station, Fichte's nation, Macaulay's empire), from the market (Bacon's use, human capital, Lyotard's performativity), or from the individual's choice (Nietzsche, Weber, Sartre, Illich, Rancière).

Modernity also contains the opposite order, and the two are at war. Rousseau and Dewey claim to fit the school to the child rather than the child to the school. But the nature they follow has also lost its end, so following nature becomes following interest, and growth has no end beyond more growth. Most modern school systems hold both at once: a bureaucratic structure that fits the child to an external purpose, and a progressive classroom that follows a nature pointing nowhere. Neither can appeal to a telos.

Fifteen modern texts in the atlas still place the end within the nature. Kant, Humboldt and Wollstonecraft do so in secular form, as the development of reason and of a person's powers. Others, from Newman and Tagore to Gandhi, Iqbal, Fazlur Rahman, al-Attas and MacIntyre, write knowingly against the split and try to restore what it removed. Whether an end can be restored once the picture of nature has lost it, or only asserted, is the question underneath the modern section.

Formation or Transmission

Three levels recur: texts and propositions, skills and habits, and a transformed self. Almost every tradition says that the third cannot be passed on directly. The Katha says the self is not won by instruction; Zhuangzi's wheelwright cannot teach his son; Augustine says no one teaches another anything; al-Ghazālī says the Sufis' knowledge is tasted and not learned; Rancière says explanation stultifies. Yet the same traditions teach, and they share a solution. They transmit the vessel, by memorizing the Veda, reciting Zhu Xi's classics or following Zarnūjī's repetitions, and trust that realization will come through practice and through living near someone who has it. Ibn Khaldūn's criticism of students who memorize and never speak, and the fate of Zhu Xi's method in the examination halls, show what happens when the vessel is mistaken for the content.

Question 3 of 7

Motive

What moves the learner?

Longing, love, curiosity, honour, reverence, will, or reward?

The second side is the learner's drive, and it decides what education can rely on for motive. Seven motives recur. Longing for release or for God moves the Upanishads, the Buddha, Augustine and al-Ghazālī's Book of Knowledge. Love of the good moves Mencius's dancing feet, the Gita's devotion, study for its own sake in Avot, and Fichte. Curiosity and delight move the Analects' love of learning, Zhuangzi, Ibn Ṭufayl, Aquinas's natural desire to know, Rousseau and Dewey. Honour and shame drive Aristotle, Miskawayh's blushing child, Quintilian's rivals and Locke's esteem and disgrace. Reverence and discipline drive Xunzi, Zhu Xi's reverent seriousness and Zarnūjī. Will and striving drive Wang Yangming, Kant, Nietzsche, Iqbal and Rancière. Reward and use drive the Lotus Sutra's carts, Bacon, Macaulay and human capital.

A motive can also change during an education. MacIntyre's child, paid in sweets to play chess, may come to want the goods of chess itself. Whether a school can make that passage when it offers only reward and the credential that stands for it is one of the questions the atlas leaves open.

Question 4 of 7

Scope

Who is educable?

Does the text open learning to all, grade learners by capacity, or restrict them by birth, sex or class?

Almost every tradition says that anyone can learn, and almost every institution restricted who did. Confucius said that in teaching there are no categories of person, Mencius that anyone can become a sage, Xunzi that any person in the street can become a Yu; the examinations that carried their teaching into office admitted only men. The Gita opens the highest goal through devotion to women, vaiśyas and śūdras, while Śaṅkara holds that śūdras may not hear the Veda. Islamic teaching makes the seeking of knowledge a duty of every Muslim, while Fārābī and Ibn Rushd reserve demonstration for the few. Aristotle wanted public education for all citizens, and citizens excluded women, slaves and those who worked with their hands.

Three grounds of restriction recur. The first is birth, in the Vedic order and its caste rules, which Ambedkar attacked as a division of labourers rather than of labour. The second is sex, which Rousseau made explicit in Book V of Emile and which Wollstonecraft answered by denying that the mind has a sex. The third is class, which runs through Montaigne's tutor and Locke's gentleman, whose poorer contemporaries were offered working schools instead. A fourth ground, capacity, is harder to judge, because it can be true. Ibn Rushd's three kinds of assent, Maimonides' concealment and Du Bois's talented tenth grade learners by what they can receive, and Rancière's whole book argues that such grading produces the incapacity it assumes.

Modernity made schooling universal and compulsory, from Prussia in 1763 to the national systems of the nineteenth century and the constitutions of the twentieth. That settled the question of scope in law and reopened it inside the school, where tracking, examinations and credentials sort pupils again. The question to put to any system is whether it sorts by capacity, by origin, or by a capacity that origin has already shaped.

Question 5 of 7

Authority

Who forms the person: teacher, tradition, state or self?

And on what is that authority grounded?

Authority comes from four places in these texts: a teacher, a tradition, the state, or the self. A teacher's authority rests on attainment, as with the Mundaka's teacher established in brahman, al-Ghazālī's shaykh and the Gita's divine charioteer. A tradition's rests on lineage and text: the chain of Pirkei Avot, the isnād and ijāza, Confucius handing on the culture of Zhou, Zhu Xi's classics. The state's begins with Aristotle's lawgiver and Fārābī's philosopher-ruler and becomes literal in the Chinese examinations, Fichte's national school and Macaulay's Minute. The self's appears early, in the Buddha's advice to the Kālāmas and in Wang Yangming, and becomes the modern default with Kant, Nietzsche, Sartre and Rancière. Before modernity the teacher is the most common source, in seventeen of thirty-four texts; after it, the self, in eleven of thirty-one. Some texts deny that a human teacher is needed at all: Ḥayy ibn Yaqẓān, Augustine's inner teacher and, in another sense, Rancière's ignorant master.

The more interesting texts limit the teacher's authority while asserting it. The Taittiriya charge tells the pupil to follow only the teacher's good acts. Mencius would rather have no Book of Documents than believe all of it. Wang Yangming would not accept even Confucius against his own heart. The Kālāma Sutta tells hearers to test for themselves, and Rabbi Meir ate the fruit and threw away the peel. The Buddha's raft, Kant's guardian who must become unnecessary and Nietzsche's educator as liberator all make the same point: a good teacher works toward the moment the student no longer needs him.

Question 6 of 7

Work

How does learning relate to livelihood and to the hand?

Is manual work part of education, beneath it, or its purpose?

The oldest division in education is between those who work with their minds and those who work with their hands. Mencius states it plainly against the agrarian Xu Xing: those who labour with their minds govern, and those who labour with their strength are governed. Aristotle says that citizens must not live a mechanical life, and that even liberal studies pursued for pay become menial. Confucius tells Fan Chi that an old farmer would teach him farming better, and calls him petty once he leaves. Newman's distinction between liberal and useful knowledge draws the same line again in the nineteenth century.

Other voices refuse the division. The rabbis require a father to teach his son a trade and praise Torah study joined to a worldly occupation, and Maimonides earned his living as a physician rather than take money for teaching. Hugh of St Victor counts the mechanical arts among the branches of knowledge. Zhuangzi gives the deepest knowledge in his book to a cook and a wheelwright. Ibn Khaldūn treats teaching itself as a craft. Wang Yangming says that scholars, farmers, artisans and merchants share the same Way. Locke wanted a gentleman to learn carpentry or gardening, and Rousseau made Emile a carpenter so that he would be independent of fortune.

Modernity changed the question from whether the learned may work with their hands to whether learning is for livelihood at all. Human capital answers yes and measures education by the earnings it adds. Gandhi answered from the other side by putting a productive craft at the centre of the school, and Washington and Du Bois argued over whether a people just out of slavery needed trades or colleges first. Ambedkar showed how a division of work by birth becomes a division of persons. Dewey tried to end the quarrel by making occupations the medium of a liberal education. Where a credential stands in for the skill, as Spence and Collins argue it often does, education reaches livelihood without passing through work at all.

Question 7 of 7

The Machine

What remains distinctively human?

Asked of texts that never asked it, so for most entries the answer is a reading forward: what, on this text's own account, could not be handed to a machine?

The seventh question is put to texts that never asked it, so for most entries the answer is a reading forward: what, on this text's own account, could not be handed to a machine? The answers converge. Almost every tradition places the deepest learning beyond what can be transmitted: realization in the Upanishads, the knack that Zhuangzi's wheelwright could not teach his son, al-Ghazālī's tasting, Augustine's inner teacher, Aristotle's practical wisdom in the particular case. If machines take over transmission, these traditions predict that what remains for human education is the harder half of the governing question, which is formation.

Two texts address the machine directly. Lyotard predicted in 1979 that the transmission of established knowledge would pass to data banks and that teachers would be needed only to teach how to use them. Dreyfus argued from Heidegger and Merleau-Ponty that skilled human knowing rests on an embodied background that cannot be written down as rules, and that expertise is learned through involvement and risk, as in apprenticeship. Systems that learn from examples rather than rules have since done much of what he said computers could not do, so his account of involvement, more than his predictions, is the part to keep.

Three others come at the question from the side. Bacon wanted a method that would leave little to the sharpness of wits and place all understandings nearly on a level, a programme machines continue. Gandhi called machinery the chief symbol of modern civilization and built his school around the hand, and Illich argued that tools past a certain scale make people their servants. Between them lies the question the atlas ends on: whether the answer to the first question, what a human being is, can still guide an education whose transmission is no longer human.

The seven questions

The Recurring Quarrels

The same few disagreements reappear across centuries and traditions. Is the learner good or bad (Mencius against Xunzi, Pelagius against Augustine, Rousseau against the Church)? Is the truth found within or in things (Wang Yangming against Zhu Xi, Augustine against Aquinas)? Does philosophy outrank revelation (Fārābī and Ibn Rushd against al-Ghazālī; Maimonides against Judah Halevi)? Are natures graded or equal (Ibn Rushd against Rancière)? Is the end within the nature or set from outside it (Aristotle and al-Attas against Bacon and human capital)? Should the school follow the child or fit him to a purpose (Rousseau and Dewey against Fichte's national school)? Should the university form the person or stay neutral (Newman and MacIntyre against Weber)? Should one enter a tradition or be emancipated from it (MacIntyre against Freire and Illich)? And in whose language should one learn (the Orientalists against Macaulay, Ngũgĩ against Achebe)? Knowing which of these quarrels a text belongs to is often the quickest way to understand it. Who may learn (Śaṅkara and Rousseau's Book V against Wollstonecraft and Ambedkar)? Is learning for livelihood or beyond it (Washington and human capital against Du Bois and Newman)? And does the hand belong in education (Aristotle and Mencius against the rabbis, Hugh of St Victor and Gandhi)?

The seven questions

What Remains Unanswered

Every tradition says the deepest thing cannot be taught, and every tradition goes on teaching it. None explains how the presence of a teacher conveys what words cannot. Ibn Khaldūn comes closest when he says habits acquired through direct contact are firmer, and the Katha when it says the teaching must come from another. Zhuangzi's wheelwright learned his knack somehow, and the text does not say how.

The question of equal or graded natures has never been settled by argument. The medieval philosophers assumed that most people need images and Rancière assumes that everyone has the same intelligence. Each assumption produces the evidence that seems to confirm it, because the learner treated as incapable often becomes so.

Whether purpose can be returned to nature after modernity separated them is also open. The traditionalist responses assert an end within the nature, but they rest on metaphysics that many of their readers no longer share. The atlas shows that the split happened and what followed from it; it does not show that the split can be undone.

Modernity has no agreed answer to the question of who sets the end of education once the state and the market do. Those who return to tradition presuppose a living community of practice that may no longer exist. Those who seek emancipation presuppose that the learner already has ends of his own. Arendt's answer, to hand on the world so that the new can renew it, works only where the world being handed on deserves to be kept.

And Lyotard's prediction of 1979 has come true. If machines take over transmission, the half of the governing question that remains is the first one: what kind of being the learner is, and what a teacher can still form in him. The household, which appears in this atlas as the father teaching Śvetaketu, Mencius's advice that fathers should not teach their own sons, and the children Rousseau left at a foundling hospital, has never had a philosophy of its own, though most education still happens there.

About This Atlas

On selection and sources. The entries were chosen to answer the governing question across traditions, not to be complete. Among those left out for length are the Ikhwān al-Ṣafāʾ, Shah Waliullah and the Deoband curriculum, Comenius, Pestalozzi, Froebel, Herbart and Montessori, the Confucian traditions of Korea and Japan, and the oral traditions of Africa beyond Ngũgĩ. Arguments are paraphrased rather than quoted. Short phrases from ancient and medieval texts are close renderings, and the translations named in each entry should be preferred.
