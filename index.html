The Governing Question

What kind of being does education presume the learner to be, and given that being, is education the formation of a person or the transmission of knowledge, toward what end, and on whose authority?

Every philosophy of education carries a picture of the human being, and that picture comes first. It decides what education is for, and the purpose then decides what is taught and who may teach it. Mencius and Xunzi disagree about the curriculum because they first disagree about human nature. So do Augustine and Pelagius, and Rousseau and the Church. The question is long because each clause depends on the one before it.

The picture is often unstated. Xunzi says outright that human nature is bad. A madrasa curriculum built on memorization, commentary and the teacher's licence to transmit (ijāza) never states its view of memory, intellect and the heart, but it implies one. Each entry therefore asks what the text says about the person and also what its practice assumes.

Modernity often reverses the order. It fixes the purpose first, whether the nation, the empire or the economy, and then assumes whatever person fits it. Macaulay's Minute of 1835 is the plainest case, and the economists' phrase ‘human capital’, coined in the early 1960s, is the general one. Putting the person first is a way of noticing the reversal when it happens.

The person

What is the human being in this view: its nature, faculties and capacities, what in it can be perfected and what corrupted? What does the text state, and what does its practice assume?

The end

Given that person, what is education for: liberation, sagehood, nearness to God, happiness, autonomy, growth, citizenship or usefulness?

What is passed on

Propositions, skill and habit, or a transformed self? And can the deepest part be passed on at all?

The teacher

Is a teacher necessary? What grounds the teacher's authority: lineage, attainment, revelation, reason, or the state?

Who presses hardest on this view, from inside the tradition or outside it?

The Traditions

A short account of each tradition's picture of the person and the argument that runs through it, so that the entries can be read in context.

India: The Self Already There

The Upanishads begin from a learner who is already, in principle, what he is to become. The self is identical with the ground of all things, and what stands in the way is ignorance, not incapacity. This explains the most striking move in the Indian material: the Mundaka classes the Vedas themselves as lower knowledge. Yet the institution built around those texts was one of the most transmission-heavy the world has known. Pupils lived in the teacher's household and memorized the Veda syllable by syllable, preserving it orally with extraordinary accuracy for three thousand years. The two fit together: memorization keeps the vessel intact, and a teacher in whom the knowledge is realized makes it live.

Buddhism changes the person. There is no hidden self to recognize, only craving and ignorance to be dissolved, and the teaching is a raft to be left behind. The Nyāya logicians give India a theory of honest and dishonest debate. The Gita places teaching inside a crisis of action. Running through all of it is a tension that is the Indian form of the question of authority: a metaphysics of universal identity coexisted with institutions that restricted who could learn, by caste and by sex.

China: Cultivating the Person

Learning (xue) is the first word of the Analects, and no tradition makes it more central to being human. The Chinese debate about the person is also the clearest anywhere. Mencius says human nature is good, Xunzi that it is bad, Gaozi that it is neither, and each view brings its own curriculum: protect the sprouts, steam and bend the wood, or carve as one pleases. The Daoists dissent from learning as such and treat it as a loss of original simplicity.

Two thousand years later, Neo-Confucianism relived the quarrel in other terms. Zhu Xi taught that one investigates principle in things and texts, and Lu Xiangshan and Wang Yangming that it is found in the heart. The institutions were academies (shuyuan) and, above all, the civil service examinations, which from the Sui dynasty to 1905 tied the cultivation of the self to selection by the state. For thirteen centuries China ran the largest experiment ever made in joining formation to an examination, and the results bear on every modern system of credentials.

Islam: Knowledge, Adab and the Heart

The Islamic tradition has the richest vocabulary for the governing question: ʿilm (knowledge), taʿlīm (instruction), tarbiya (rearing), taʾdīb (formation in adab), and for the person fiṭra (the original disposition), ʿaql (intellect), qalb (heart) and nafs (the lower self). It also has two inheritances. One is prophetic and legal, carried by chains of transmission (isnād) and by the personal licence (ijāza) a teacher gives a student to teach a text. The other is Greek, received through Fārābī and Miskawayh, in which the person is a rational soul whose intellect must be actualized and whose natures differ, so that teaching must be graded. Ibn Rushd's three kinds of assent and Ibn Ṭufayl's solitary philosopher belong to that line.

Al-Ghazālī joined the legal, theological and Sufi strands into a single account in which knowledge is for action and the heart is a mirror to be polished under a shaykh. The madrasa, spread by the Nizāmiyya colleges from 1067, gave the tradition its institution, and Zarnūjī's manual its student's handbook. Ibn Khaldūn then described the whole system as a craft that forms habits in a civilization, with a naturalism no other pre-modern tradition matched. The question of authority runs through the tradition in three forms: the chain of transmission, the dispute with the Taʿlīmiyya over an infallible teacher, and the philosophers' practice of writing in concealment.

Judaism and Christianity: Study and the Inner Teacher

These are two models, and neither simply follows the Islamic one. In the medieval period Maimonides, Ibn Rushd and Aquinas share Aristotle and read one another, and the questions of graded teaching and concealment are common to all three. But rabbinic Judaism made study itself a form of worship and argument a form of study. It handed down the Torah through a chain of teachers and paired every student with a companion. It preserved the losing opinion beside the winning one, as Islamic law preserved the disagreements of its schools.

Christianity added a different problem. For Augustine the learner is fallen and the only true teacher is inward, so the question of the teacher becomes a question about grace. Monastic reading, Hugh of St Victor's restoration of the divine image, and Aquinas's teacher who assists nature as a doctor does are three answers to it. The universities of Paris and Bologna, born around 1200, are the institution that the modern section inherits.

Greece and Rome: Habit and the Speaker

Plato is covered in the Socratic atlas. The Greek word for this whole subject, paideia, meant upbringing, culture and education at once. Two lines came out of Athens and still shape Western schooling. The philosophical line ran through Aristotle, for whom virtue is a habit completed by practical wisdom and education the business of the lawgiver. The rhetorical line ran from Isocrates to Quintilian, for whom the educated person is a good man skilled in speaking. The medieval liberal arts combined them, and Montaigne, at the edge of modernity, turned both toward the forming of judgment.

Modernity: The Order Reversed

From Locke onward the learner is pictured either as blank paper or as a rational being who must grow into autonomy. Responsibility for formation passes from churches and households to the state, which begins to require schooling: Prussia's general school regulations of 1763 are an early landmark. The university justifies itself first by Humboldt's story of self-formation and then by the story of a people's emancipation. Weber expels formation from the lecture hall, and Lyotard reports that both stories have lost their hold and that knowledge is now judged by its performance.

For the colonized world modernity arrived as Macaulay's Minute: an education designed for the needs of an empire, with a person assumed to fit them. In South Asia it produced the lasting split between madrasa and school that Sir Syed, Iqbal and Fazlur Rahman tried in different ways to heal. In its sharpest form the reversal is the doctrine of human capital, in which the economy sets the purpose of education and the learner is valued by his expected return. The responses that follow fall into three groups. Newman, al-Attas and MacIntyre return to formation within a tradition. Freire, Illich and Rancière seek emancipation from the teacher and the school. Arendt defends the teacher's authority as responsibility for an old world handed to newcomers. Ngũgĩ adds that all of this happens in a language, and asks whose.

Across the Traditions

The Person

Seven pictures of the learner recur, and each brings its own idea of what a teacher does. If the learner is whole but veiled, as in the Upanishads, Śaṅkara, Augustine, al-Ghazālī, Zhu Xi and Wang Yangming, the teacher removes what hides the truth, and the deepest knowledge is recognition. If the learner is good by nature, as for Mencius, the Daoists, Rousseau and Tagore, the teacher protects and must above all not force. If the learner is unformed, as for Aristotle, the Xueji, Miskawayh, Ibn Khaldūn, Aquinas, Locke and Dewey, the teacher habituates, and education is the forming of a second nature. If the learner is wayward, as for Xunzi and the rabbis of the two inclinations, the teacher straightens, and ritual or Torah is the press-frame. If the learner is bound, as in Buddhism and the Gita, the teacher releases. If the learner is free, as for Kant, Nietzsche, Freire and Rancière, the teacher emancipates and must work to become unnecessary. And if the learner is a resource, as Macaulay and Lyotard describe, the teacher fits him to a purpose set elsewhere.

The most surprising agreements cross traditions. The Upanishadic self beneath names and forms, Augustine's inner teacher, al-Ghazālī's rusted mirror and Wang Yangming's innate knowing are versions of one view, in which the truth is within and veiled. Aristotle's hexis, Ibn Khaldūn's malaka, the Xueji's uncarved jade and Dewey's plasticity are versions of another. The deepest disagreement is about equality. Medieval philosophers in all three Abrahamic traditions, and Śaṅkara with his qualified pupil, assume that natures differ and teaching must be graded. Confucius, who said that in teaching there are no categories of person, Mencius, for whom anyone can become a sage, and Rancière, for whom intelligence is equal, assume the opposite. Neither side proves its assumption.

The End

Pre-modern traditions place the end of education beyond the learner's present self: liberation in India, sagehood in China, nearness to God in Islam, Judaism and Christianity, happiness as the excellent activity of the soul in Greece. Each of these ends also defines the person, which is why the order of the governing question holds for them. Modern ends are of two kinds. Autonomy (Kant), Bildung (Humboldt), growth (Dewey) and liberation (Freire) still look to the person. The ends that set modern school systems in practice, national strength, imperial administration and economic return, look elsewhere and assume a person to suit them. That is the reversal, and most of the modern section is an argument about it.

What Is Passed On

Three levels recur: texts and propositions, skills and habits, and a transformed self. Almost every tradition says that the third cannot be passed on directly. The Katha says the self is not won by instruction; Zhuangzi's wheelwright cannot teach his son; Augustine says no one teaches another anything; al-Ghazālī says the Sufis' knowledge is tasted and not learned; Rancière says explanation stultifies. Yet the same traditions teach, and they share a solution. They transmit the vessel, by memorizing the Veda, reciting Zhu Xi's classics or following Zarnūjī's repetitions, and trust that realization will come through practice and through living near someone who has it. Ibn Khaldūn's criticism of students who memorize and never speak, and the fate of Zhu Xi's method in the examination halls, show what happens when the vessel is mistaken for the content.

The Teacher

Authority has five sources in these texts. Lineage grounds it in the chains of Pirkei Avot, the isnād and the sampradāya; attainment in the Mundaka's teacher established in brahman and al-Ghazālī's shaykh; revelation in the Prophet and in Krishna; reason in Kant; and the state in Macaulay's Minute and the Chinese examinations. Some texts deny that a human teacher is needed at all: Ḥayy ibn Yaqẓān, Augustine's inner teacher and, in another sense, Rancière's ignorant master.

The more interesting texts limit the teacher's authority while asserting it. The Taittiriya charge tells the pupil to follow only the teacher's good acts. Mencius would rather have no Book of Documents than believe all of it. Wang Yangming would not accept even Confucius against his own heart. The Kālāma Sutta tells hearers to test for themselves, and Rabbi Meir ate the fruit and threw away the peel. The Buddha's raft, Kant's guardian who must become unnecessary and Nietzsche's educator as liberator all make the same point: a good teacher works toward the moment the student no longer needs him.

The Recurring Quarrels

The same few disagreements reappear across centuries and traditions. Is the learner good or bad (Mencius against Xunzi, Pelagius against Augustine, Rousseau against the Church)? Is the truth found within or in things (Wang Yangming against Zhu Xi, Augustine against Aquinas)? Does philosophy outrank revelation (Fārābī and Ibn Rushd against al-Ghazālī; Maimonides against Judah Halevi)? Are natures graded or equal (Ibn Rushd against Rancière)? Should the university form the person or stay neutral (Newman and MacIntyre against Weber)? Should one enter a tradition or be emancipated from it (MacIntyre against Freire and Illich)? And in whose language should one learn (the Orientalists against Macaulay, Ngũgĩ against Achebe)? Knowing which of these quarrels a text belongs to is often the quickest way to understand it.

What Remains Unanswered

Every tradition says the deepest thing cannot be taught, and every tradition goes on teaching it. None explains how the presence of a teacher conveys what words cannot. Ibn Khaldūn comes closest when he says habits acquired through direct contact are firmer, and the Katha when it says the teaching must come from another. Zhuangzi's wheelwright learned his knack somehow, and the text does not say how.

The question of equal or graded natures has never been settled by argument. The medieval philosophers assumed that most people need images and Rancière assumes that everyone has the same intelligence. Each assumption produces the evidence that seems to confirm it, because the learner treated as incapable often becomes so.

Modernity has no agreed answer to the question of who sets the end of education once the state and the market do. Those who return to tradition presuppose a living community of practice that may no longer exist. Those who seek emancipation presuppose that the learner already has ends of his own. Arendt's answer, to hand on the world so that the new can renew it, works only where the world being handed on deserves to be kept.

And Lyotard's prediction of 1979 has come true. If machines take over transmission, the half of the governing question that remains is the first one: what kind of being the learner is, and what a teacher can still form in him. The household, which appears in this atlas as the father teaching Śvetaketu, Mencius's advice that fathers should not teach their own sons, and the children Rousseau left at a foundling hospital, has never had a philosophy of its own, though most education still happens there.

On selection and sources. The entries were chosen to answer the governing question across traditions, not to be complete. Among those left out for length are the Ikhwān al-Ṣafāʾ, Shah Waliullah and the Deoband curriculum, Comenius, Pestalozzi, Froebel, Herbart and Montessori, Gandhi's Nai Talim as a separate entry, the Confucian traditions of Korea and Japan, and the oral traditions of Africa beyond Ngũgĩ. Arguments are paraphrased rather than quoted. Short phrases from ancient and medieval texts are close renderings, and the translations named in each entry should be preferred. The placement of each text among the seven pictures of the learner is an interpretation and is meant to be contested in the readings.
